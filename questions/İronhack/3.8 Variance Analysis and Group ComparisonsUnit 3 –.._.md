# Variance Analysis and Group Comparisons

## Introduction

In the previous lesson, you learned to measure how two continuous variables relate to each other using correlation. But many important questions in data science involve comparing groups rather than measuring continuous associations. Does a new drug treatment produce better outcomes than the existing one? Do customers from different marketing channels spend different amounts? Do three different recommendation algorithms produce meaningfully different click-through rates?

These are fundamentally questions about whether the differences you observe between groups are real or just the result of random variation. Variance analysis gives you the framework to answer them. The core insight is elegant: if the groups are truly different, the variation between groups should be large relative to the variation within each group. If the groups are essentially the same, the between-group variation will be small, and most of the total variation will come from individual differences within groups.

This lesson covers one-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance), the multiple comparisons problem that arises when you test many groups, effect sizes that tell you whether statistically significant differences are practically meaningful, and the connection between variance explained and model evaluation in machine learning.

## Within-Group vs. Between-Group Variance

The fundamental idea behind ANOVA is the decomposition of total variance into two components. Imagine you have three groups of students who used different study methods, and you measured their exam scores. The total spread in scores comes from two sources.

* **Within-group variance** captures how much individuals vary within each group. Even within the same study method group, some students do better than others due to differences in ability, motivation, sleep, and countless other factors. This is the "noise floor" of individual variation.
* **Between-group variance** captures how much the group means differ from the overall mean. If the study methods actually matter, the group means will be spread apart. If the methods make no difference, the group means will cluster near the overall mean.


```
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

np.random.seed(42)

method_a = np.random.normal(72, 8, 40)
method_b = np.random.normal(78, 8, 40)
method_c = np.random.normal(75, 8, 40)

scores = np.concatenate([method_a, method_b, method_c])
groups = ['A'] * 40 + ['B'] * 40 + ['C'] * 40
df = pd.DataFrame({'score': scores, 'method': groups})

overall_mean = scores.mean()
group_means = df.groupby('method')['score'].mean()

print(f"Overall mean: {overall_mean:.2f}")
print(f"\nGroup means:")
print(group_means.round(2))
```

Now let's compute the variance components manually to build intuition:


```
n_per_group = 40
k = 3

ss_between = sum(n_per_group * (group_means[g] - overall_mean) ** 2 for g in ['A', 'B', 'C'])
ss_within = sum(
    ((df[df['method'] == g]['score'] - group_means[g]) ** 2).sum()
    for g in ['A', 'B', 'C'])

ss_total = ((scores - overall_mean) ** 2).sum()

print(f"SS Between: {ss_between:.2f}")
print(f"SS Within:  {ss_within:.2f}")
print(f"SS Total:   {ss_total:.2f}")
print(f"SS Between + SS Within = {ss_between + ss_within:.2f} (should equal SS Total)")
```

The beauty of this decomposition is that $SS_{\text{Total}} = SS_{\text{Between}} + SS_{\text{Within}}$. All of the variation in the data is accounted for by these two sources. ANOVA asks: is the between-group component large enough, relative to the within-group component, to conclude that the groups are genuinely different?

## One-Way ANOVA: The F-Test

The formal ANOVA test converts the variance decomposition into a test statistic called $F$. The $F$-statistic is the ratio of between-group variance to within-group variance, adjusted for degrees of freedom:

$$F = \frac{SS_{\text{between}} / (k - 1)}{SS_{\text{within}} / (N - k)}$$

Where $k$ is the number of groups and $N$ is the total sample size. The numerator is the mean square between groups, and the denominator is the mean square within groups.

If the groups are truly identical (the null hypothesis), $F$ should be close to 1 because between-group variance would just be sampling noise, similar in magnitude to within-group variance. The larger the $F$-statistic, the more evidence we have that the groups differ.


```
ms_between = ss_between / (k - 1)
ms_within = ss_within / (len(scores) - k)
f_statistic = ms_between / ms_within

print(f"Mean Square Between: {ms_between:.2f}")
print(f"Mean Square Within:  {ms_within:.2f}")
print(f"F-statistic: {f_statistic:.4f}")
```

Scipy provides a one-liner that does the same calculation:


```
f_stat, p_value = stats.f_oneway(method_a, method_b, method_c)
print(f"F-statistic: {f_stat:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.6f}")
```

If the p-value is below your chosen significance level (commonly 0.05), you reject the null hypothesis and conclude that at least one group mean is significantly different from the others. Note the important nuance: ANOVA tells you that the groups are not all the same, but it does not tell you which specific groups differ. For that, you need post-hoc tests.

### Assumptions of ANOVA

ANOVA relies on three assumptions, and understanding them helps you decide when it is appropriate and when you need alternatives:

1. **Independence:** Observations within and across groups are independent. One student's score does not influence another's. This is usually ensured by experimental design.
2. **Normality:** The data within each group should be approximately normally distributed. ANOVA is reasonably robust to violations of this assumption, especially with moderate to large sample sizes (thanks to the central limit theorem), but severely skewed data can cause problems.
3. **Homogeneity of variances:** The variance within each group should be roughly equal. If one group is much more variable than the others, the $F$-test can be misleading. Levene's test checks this assumption:


```
levene_stat, levene_p = stats.levene(method_a, method_b, method_c)
print(f"Levene's test: statistic={levene_stat:.4f}, p={levene_p:.4f}")
```

If Levene's test is significant, the equal variance assumption is violated, and you should use Welch's ANOVA instead, which does not assume equal variances.

## Post-Hoc Tests: Finding Which Groups Differ

When ANOVA rejects the null hypothesis, you know that at least one group is different, but not which one. Post-hoc tests perform pairwise comparisons while controlling for the increased risk of false positives.

### The Multiple Comparisons Problem

If you compare three groups with simple t-tests, you need three comparisons (A vs B, A vs C, B vs C). Each test has a 5% chance of a false positive. The probability of at least one false positive across all three tests is:

$$1 - (1 - 0.05)^3 \approx 0.143$$

That is a 14.3% chance of at least one false alarm, which is nearly three times your intended error rate. With 10 groups, you would make 45 pairwise comparisons, and the probability of at least one false positive exceeds 90%. This is why post-hoc corrections are essential.


```
from itertools import combinations

n_groups = [3, 5, 10, 20]
alpha = 0.05

for k in n_groups:
    n_comparisons = k * (k - 1) // 2
    familywise_error = 1 - (1 - alpha) ** n_comparisons
    print(f"{k} groups → {n_comparisons} comparisons → "          f"familywise error rate: {familywise_error:.3f}")
```

### Tukey's HSD (Honestly Significant Difference)

Tukey's HSD is the most common post-hoc test for ANOVA. It controls the familywise error rate by using the studentized range distribution to determine how far apart two group means must be to be considered significantly different.


```
from statsmodels.stats.multicomp import pairwise_tukeyhsd

tukey = pairwise_tukeyhsd(df['score'], df['method'], alpha=0.05)
print(tukey)
```

The output shows each pairwise comparison with the mean difference, confidence interval, and whether the difference is statistically significant. This gives you the specific answer ANOVA could not: which groups differ from which.

### Bonferroni Correction

A simpler (but more conservative) approach is the Bonferroni correction: divide your significance level by the number of comparisons. If you are making 3 comparisons and want a familywise error rate of 0.05, each individual test must use $\alpha = 0.05/3 \approx 0.0167$.


```
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind

pairs = [('A', 'B'), ('A', 'C'), ('B', 'C')]
n_comparisons = len(pairs)
bonferroni_alpha = 0.05 / n_comparisons

for g1, g2 in pairs:
    data1 = df[df['method'] == g1]['score']
    data2 = df[df['method'] == g2]['score']
    t_stat, p_val = ttest_ind(data1, data2)
    significant = "Yes" if p_val < bonferroni_alpha else "No"
    print(f"{g1} vs {g2}: t={t_stat:.3f}, p={p_val:.4f}, "          f"significant at Bonferroni α={bonferroni_alpha:.4f}? {significant}")
```

Bonferroni is simple and widely applicable, but it becomes very conservative with many comparisons. Tukey's HSD is generally preferred for ANOVA follow-ups because it is specifically designed for comparing all pairs of group means.

## Effect Sizes: Beyond Statistical Significance

A statistically significant ANOVA result tells you that the group differences are unlikely to be zero. But that does not tell you whether the differences are large enough to matter in practice. Effect sizes fill this gap.

### Cohen's d for Pairwise Comparisons

Cohen's d measures the difference between two group means in terms of standard deviations. It tells you how far apart the groups are, relative to their variability.


```
def cohens_d(group1, group2):
    n1, n2 = len(group1), len(group2)
    var1, var2 = group1.var(ddof=1), group2.var(ddof=1)
    pooled_std = np.sqrt(((n1 - 1) * var1 + (n2 - 1) * var2) / (n1 + n2 - 2))
    return (group1.mean() - group2.mean()) / pooled_std

d_ab = cohens_d(method_a, method_b)
d_ac = cohens_d(method_a, method_c)
d_bc = cohens_d(method_b, method_c)

print(f"Cohen's d (A vs B): {d_ab:.4f}")
print(f"Cohen's d (A vs C): {d_ac:.4f}")
print(f"Cohen's d (B vs C): {d_bc:.4f}")
```

Cohen's guidelines for interpreting d:

* $|d| \approx 0.2$: Small effect
* $|d| \approx 0.5$: Medium effect
* $|d| \approx 0.8$: Large effect

These are rough benchmarks. The practical importance of an effect depends on context. A "small" effect on customer retention might be worth millions of dollars for a large company, while a "large" effect on a metric nobody cares about is irrelevant.

### Eta-Squared ($\eta^2$) for Overall Effect

While Cohen's d works for pairs, eta-squared tells you how much of the total variance in the data is explained by the group membership. It is the ANOVA equivalent of $R^2$ in regression.


```
eta_squared = ss_between / ss_total
print(f"Eta-squared: {eta_squared:.4f}")
print(f"Interpretation: {eta_squared * 100:.1f}% of variance in scores "      f"is explained by study method")
```

Eta-squared benchmarks:

* $\eta^2 \approx 0.01$: Small effect
* $\eta^2 \approx 0.06$: Medium effect
* $\eta^2 \approx 0.14$: Large effect

Notice the connection to machine learning: eta-squared is conceptually identical to $R^2$ in regression. Both measure the proportion of variance in the outcome that is explained by the predictor(s). When you compute $R^2$ for a regression model, you are doing the same decomposition as ANOVA, just in a different framework.

## Variance Explained in Machine Learning

The connection between ANOVA and ML evaluation is deeper than it might appear. Several key ML concepts are fundamentally about variance decomposition.

### $R^2$ as Variance Explained

In regression, $R^2$ measures how much of the target's variance your model captures:


```
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score

np.random.seed(42)
X = np.random.normal(0, 1, (200, 3))
y = 2 * X[:, 0] - 1.5 * X[:, 1] + 0.5 * X[:, 2] + np.random.normal(0, 1, 200)

model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)
y_pred = model.predict(X)
r2 = r2_score(y, y_pred)

print(f"R² = {r2:.4f}")
print(f"Interpretation: The model explains {r2 * 100:.1f}% of the variance in y")
```

An $R^2$ of 0.85 means 85% of the variance in y is accounted for by the model. The remaining 15% is unexplained variance, which could be irreducible noise, missing features, or nonlinear patterns the model cannot capture.

### Feature Importance as Variance Contribution

In tree-based models, feature importance is often measured by how much each feature contributes to reducing variance (or impurity) in the target:


```
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor

rf = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=100, random_state=42)
rf.fit(X, y)

importance = pd.Series(rf.feature_importances_, index=['x1', 'x2', 'x3'])
print("Feature importances (variance reduction):")
print(importance.sort_values(ascending=False).round(4))
```

These importances sum to 1 and represent each feature's share of the total variance reduction achieved by the model. This is the same idea as ANOVA: how much does knowing this variable (or group) reduce the unexplained variance?

## Non-Parametric Alternatives

When ANOVA's assumptions are violated and cannot be fixed through transformations, non-parametric alternatives provide a way to compare groups without assuming normality.

### Kruskal-Wallis Test

The Kruskal-Wallis test is the non-parametric equivalent of one-way ANOVA. Instead of comparing means, it ranks all observations and checks whether the average ranks differ across groups. It tests whether the groups come from the same distribution.


```
np.random.seed(42)
group_x = np.random.exponential(5, 30)
group_y = np.random.exponential(8, 30)
group_z = np.random.exponential(5.5, 30)

h_stat, p_value = stats.kruskal(group_x, group_y, group_z)
print(f"Kruskal-Wallis H: {h_stat:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.6f}")
```

### Mann-Whitney U Test

For pairwise comparisons (two groups), the Mann-Whitney U test is the non-parametric equivalent of the independent t-test:


```
u_stat, p_value = stats.mannwhitneyu(group_x, group_y, alternative='two-sided')
print(f"Mann-Whitney U: {u_stat:.4f}")
print(f"p-value: {p_value:.6f}")
```

### Choosing Your Test

Deciding which test to use depends on your data and your question:

* Two groups, normal data, equal variances: Independent t-test
* Two groups, normal data, unequal variances: Welch's t-test
* Two groups, non-normal data or ordinal: Mann-Whitney U test
* Three+ groups, normal data, equal variances: One-way ANOVA + Tukey's HSD
* Three+ groups, normal data, unequal variances: Welch's ANOVA
* Three+ groups, non-normal data or ordinal: Kruskal-Wallis + Dunn's test


```
def choose_test(data_groups, alpha=0.05):
    """Suggest an appropriate statistical test based on data properties."""
    k = len(data_groups)

    normality_ok = all(
        stats.shapiro(g)[1] > alpha for g in data_groups if len(g) >= 8
    )
    equal_var = stats.levene(*data_groups)[1] > alpha if normality_ok else None

    if k == 2:
        if normality_ok and equal_var:
            return "Independent t-test"
        elif normality_ok:
            return "Welch's t-test"
        else:
            return "Mann-Whitney U test"
    else:
        if normality_ok and equal_var:
            return "One-way ANOVA + Tukey's HSD"
        elif normality_ok:
            return "Welch's ANOVA"
        else:
            return "Kruskal-Wallis + Dunn's test"

recommendation = choose_test([method_a, method_b, method_c])
print(f"Recommended test: {recommendation}")
```

## ANOVA with Statsmodels: A More Detailed Approach

For a more comprehensive ANOVA analysis with a proper ANOVA table, statsmodels provides a formula-based interface:


```
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.formula.api import ols

model = ols('score ~ C(method)', data=df).fit()
anova_table = sm.stats.anova_lm(model, typ=2)
print(anova_table)
```

This produces a standard ANOVA table with sum of squares, degrees of freedom, F-statistic, and p-value. The `C(method)` notation tells statsmodels to treat method as a categorical variable.

You can also extract model diagnostics:


```
print(f"\nModel R²: {model.rsquared:.4f}")
print(f"Adjusted R²: {model.rsquared_adj:.4f}")
print(f"\nGroup means from model:")
print(model.params)
```

The $R^2$ from this regression is exactly the eta-squared we computed earlier. The ANOVA F-test and the regression F-test are mathematically identical when the predictor is a categorical variable.

## Practical Example: A/B/C Testing in Product Analytics

Let's put everything together with a realistic scenario. You run an A/B/C test with three different landing page designs and measure conversion rates:


```
np.random.seed(42)

design_a = np.random.binomial(1, 0.12, 500)
design_b = np.random.binomial(1, 0.15, 500)
design_c = np.random.binomial(1, 0.13, 500)

test_df = pd.DataFrame({
    'converted': np.concatenate([design_a, design_b, design_c]),
    'design': ['A'] * 500 + ['B'] * 500 + ['C'] * 500,
})

conversion_rates = test_df.groupby('design')['converted'].mean()
print("Conversion rates:")
print(conversion_rates.round(4))

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

contingency = pd.crosstab(test_df['design'], test_df['converted'])
chi2, p_value, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(contingency)
print(f"\nChi-squared test: χ²={chi2:.4f}, p={p_value:.4f}")
```

For binary outcomes, you use a chi-squared test rather than ANOVA (since the data is not normally distributed). But the conceptual framework is the same: are the differences between groups larger than what you would expect from chance?


```
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

conversions = [design_a.sum(), design_b.sum()]
nobs = [len(design_a), len(design_b)]
z_stat, p_val = proportions_ztest(conversions, nobs, alternative='two-sided')
print(f"\nA vs B proportions z-test: z={z_stat:.4f}, p={p_val:.4f}")

effect = conversion_rates['B'] - conversion_rates['A']
relative_lift = effect / conversion_rates['A'] * 100
print(f"Absolute effect: {effect:.4f}")
print(f"Relative lift: {relative_lift:.1f}%")
```

In product analytics, the practical question is not just "is the difference significant?" but "is the difference large enough to justify the effort of implementing Design B?" This is where effect sizes and business context become essential.

## Summary

Variance analysis is the bridge between descriptive statistics ("these group means look different") and inferential statistics ("these groups are actually different beyond what chance would produce"). In this lesson, you have learned:

* Within-group variance reflects individual differences; between-group variance reflects group-level effects. ANOVA compares these two sources of variation.
* One-way ANOVA uses the F-statistic to test whether at least one group mean differs significantly. It assumes independence, normality, and equal variances.
* Multiple comparisons inflate false positive rates. Post-hoc tests like Tukey's HSD and corrections like Bonferroni control this risk.
* Effect sizes (Cohen's d for pairs, eta-squared for overall) tell you whether significant differences are practically meaningful, not just statistically detectable.
* Variance explained connects directly to ML evaluation: eta-squared in ANOVA is conceptually the same as $R^2$ in regression and feature importance in tree models.
* Non-parametric alternatives (Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney) extend group comparison to situations where parametric assumptions fail.
