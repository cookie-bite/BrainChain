# Descriptive Statistics as Data Language

## Introduction

Before you build a model, run a test, or draw a conclusion, you need to describe what you have. Descriptive statistics are the vocabulary of data science: a compact set of numbers that let you summarize thousands or millions of observations in a way that humans can reason about. When someone says "the average customer spends $47 per visit" or "response times have a long right tail," they are using descriptive statistics to compress raw data into meaning.

This lesson walks you through the core toolkit of descriptive statistics: how to classify the data you are working with, how to measure its center and spread, how to describe its shape, and how to detect when summary numbers are hiding important details. Along the way, you will write Python code that computes these summaries on real-ish data and visualizes the results. By the end, you will be able to look at a dataset and give a precise, honest description of what it contains, and just as importantly, you will know when a single summary number is not enough.

## Types of Data

The first step in describing data is knowing what kind of data you are dealing with. The type determines which summaries make sense, which visualizations work, and which statistical methods apply. Getting this wrong at the start leads to nonsense results downstream.

### Numeric vs. Categorical

The most fundamental split in data types is between numeric data (also called quantitative) and categorical data (also called qualitative).

* **Numeric data:** Represents quantities. It answers the question "how much?" or "how many?" Examples include temperature, revenue, age, and number of purchases. You can do arithmetic on numeric data: adding, averaging, and computing differences all make sense.
* **Categorical data:** Represents group membership. It answers the question "which type?" or "which category?" Examples include country, product category, customer segment, and yes/no flags. Arithmetic on categorical data is meaningless. The "average" of France, Germany, and Spain is not a thing.


```
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": [101, 102, 103, 104, 105],
    "age": [28, 35, 42, 31, 55],
    "annual_spend": [1200.50, 3400.00, 780.25, 5100.75, 2200.00],
    "membership_tier": ["Silver", "Gold", "Silver", "Platinum", "Gold"],
    "is_active": [True, True, False, True, False]
})

print(df.dtypes)
```

In this example, `age` and `annual_spend` are numeric. `membership_tier` is categorical. `is_active` is categorical too, even though pandas stores it as boolean. The column `customer_id` looks numeric but is actually an identifier, not a quantity. You would never compute the "average customer ID." Recognizing these distinctions early prevents mistakes that are misleading or incorrect.

### Discrete vs. Continuous

Within numeric data, there is a further distinction between discrete and continuous.

* **Discrete data:** Takes on countable values, often integers. Number of children, number of website visits, and number of defective items in a batch are all discrete. There is no such thing as 2.7 children.
* **Continuous data:** Can take any value within a range, including fractions and decimals. Height, weight, temperature, and time are continuous. Between any two measurements, there are infinitely many possible values.

This distinction matters because it affects which probability distributions apply and which visualizations are appropriate. Bar charts work well for discrete data; histograms work well for continuous data.

### Ordinal vs. Nominal

Categorical data splits further into ordinal and nominal.

* **Nominal categories:** Have no natural ordering. Colors, countries, and product names are nominal. There is no sense in which "red" is greater than "blue."
* **Ordinal categories:** Have a meaningful order but the distances between categories are not necessarily equal. Education level (high school, bachelor's, master's, PhD), customer satisfaction (1-5 stars), and T-shirt sizes (S, M, L, XL) are ordinal. You know that "Gold" is above "Silver" in a membership tier, but you cannot say that the gap between Silver and Gold is the same as the gap between Gold and Platinum.


```
tier_order = pd.CategoricalDtype(categories=["Silver", "Gold", "Platinum"], ordered=True)
df["membership_tier"] = df["membership_tier"].astype(tier_order)

print(df["membership_tier"].min())
print(df["membership_tier"].max())
```

Pandas supports ordered categoricals, which lets you compute min and max on ordinal data. This would not make sense for nominal data like product names.

## Measuring the Center

Once you know what kind of data you have, the next question is usually: "What is a typical value?" Measures of central tendency answer this question, but they do it in different ways, and choosing the wrong one can paint a seriously misleading picture.

### Mean: The Balance Point

The arithmetic mean is the sum of all values divided by the count. It is the number that "balances" the distribution, like the fulcrum of a seesaw.


```
import numpy as np

spend = df["annual_spend"]
mean_spend = spend.mean()

print(f"Mean annual spend: ${mean_spend:,.2f}")
# Equivalent with numpy
print(f"NumPy mean: ${np.mean(spend):,.2f}")
```

The mean is the most commonly used average, and for good reason: it uses every data point, it has nice mathematical properties, and it is the basis for many statistical methods. But it has a critical weakness: it is sensitive to extreme values.

Imagine a company where five employees earn $50k, $55k, $60k, $52k, and $48k. The mean salary is $53k, which feels like a reasonable summary. Now add the CEO at $2,000,000. The mean jumps to $377,500, a number that describes nobody in the company. The mean got pulled toward the extreme value, which is exactly what it is designed to do, but that mathematical correctness creates a misleading summary.

### Median: The Middle Value

The median is the value that splits the data in half: 50% of observations fall below it, and 50% fall above. Unlike the mean, the median is not affected by extreme values. In the salary example above, the median stays at $53,500 whether the CEO earns $200k or $200 million.


```
median_spend = spend.median()
print(f"Median annual spend: ${median_spend:,.2f}")
print(f"NumPy median: ${np.median(spend):,.2f}")
```

The median is a robust statistic, meaning it is resistant to outliers and skewed distributions. This makes it the better choice when your data has heavy tails, extreme values, or strong asymmetry. Income distributions, home prices, and response times are classic cases where the median is more informative than the mean.

### Mode: The Most Frequent Value

The mode is the value that appears most often. For categorical data, the mode is often the only measure of central tendency that makes sense. You cannot compute the mean of a set of colors, but you can say that "blue" is the most popular one.


```
print(f"Most common tier: {df['membership_tier'].mode()[0]}")

# For numeric data, mode is less useful but still valid
ages = pd.Series([25, 30, 30, 35, 40, 30, 45])
print(f"Modal age: {ages.mode()[0]}")
```

For continuous data, the mode is rarely useful because exact duplicates are uncommon. A dataset of 10,000 precise temperature readings might have no repeated values at all. In that case, people sometimes talk about the "modal region" where the density peaks, which is really a histogram concept.

### When to Use Which

Here is a practical decision framework:

* **Symmetric data, no outliers:** Mean and median will be close. Use the mean for its mathematical convenience.
* **Skewed data or outliers present:** Prefer the median. Report the mean too if you want, but flag the skew.
* **Categorical data:** Use the mode. Mean and median do not apply.
* **Ordinal data:** Median is appropriate (it only requires ordering). Mean is debatable.


```
# Quick comparison
print(df[["age", "annual_spend"]].agg(["mean", "median"]))
```

When the mean and median diverge significantly, that is a signal worth investigating. It usually indicates skewness or the presence of outliers, both of which deserve attention before you proceed with analysis.

## Measuring Spread

Knowing the center is only half the story. Two datasets can have the same mean and median but look completely different. Measures of spread (also called dispersion or variability) tell you how tightly the data clusters around the center, or how wildly it scatters.

### Variance and Standard Deviation

The variance measures the average squared distance from the mean. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, which brings it back to the original units.


```
# Population variance and std (ddof=0) vs sample (ddof=1)
print(f"Sample std of spend: ${spend.std():,.2f}")
print(f"Population std of spend: ${spend.std(ddof=0):,.2f}")

print(f"Sample variance: {spend.var():,.2f}")
print(f"NumPy std (ddof=0 default): {np.std(spend):,.2f}")
print(f"NumPy std (ddof=1): {np.std(spend, ddof=1):,.2f}")
```

> **A quick note on ddof:** When you compute variance or standard deviation from a sample (which is almost always), you should use `ddof=1` (the default in pandas). This corrects for the fact that a sample tends to underestimate the true population variance. NumPy defaults to `ddof=0`, which gives you the population formula. This is a common source of subtle bugs when mixing pandas and numpy.

The standard deviation has a nice intuitive interpretation: for roughly bell-shaped distributions, about 68% of data falls within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. This is the empirical rule (or 68-95-99.7 rule), and while it only applies precisely to normal distributions, it is a useful mental model for many real-world datasets.

### Range and Interquartile Range (IQR)

The range is the simplest measure of spread: maximum minus minimum. It is easy to compute but extremely sensitive to outliers. A single extreme value stretches the range dramatically.

The interquartile range (IQR) is the difference between the 75th percentile (Q3) and the 25th percentile (Q1). It captures the spread of the middle 50% of the data, ignoring the tails entirely.


```
Q1 = spend.quantile(0.25)
Q3 = spend.quantile(0.75)
IQR = Q3 - Q1

print(f"Range: ${spend.max() - spend.min():,.2f}")
print(f"Q1: ${Q1:,.2f}")
print(f"Q3: ${Q3:,.2f}")
print(f"IQR: ${IQR:,.2f}")
```

The IQR is robust to outliers, just like the median. In fact, the pair (median, IQR) is a robust alternative to (mean, standard deviation) when your data is skewed or has extreme values.

### Coefficient of Variation

When you need to compare the variability of datasets measured in different units or on very different scales, the coefficient of variation (CV) is useful. It is the standard deviation divided by the mean, expressed as a percentage.


```
cv_spend = (spend.std() / spend.mean()) * 100
cv_age = (df["age"].std() / df["age"].mean()) * 100

print(f"CV of spend: {cv_spend:.1f}%")
print(f"CV of age: {cv_age:.1f}%")
```

A CV of 60% tells you the standard deviation is 60% of the mean, which indicates high relative variability. A CV of 10% suggests the data is tightly clustered around the mean. This is particularly useful in fields like finance, where you might want to compare the volatility of assets with very different price levels.

## Quantiles, Percentiles, and Z-Scores

Beyond the center and spread, you often need to locate specific positions within a distribution. Quantiles, percentiles, and z-scores are the tools for this.

### Quantiles and Percentiles

A percentile tells you the value below which a given percentage of observations fall. If your score is at the 90th percentile, 90% of scores are below yours. Percentiles and quantiles are essentially the same concept with different scales: the 25th percentile is the 0.25 quantile.


```
percentiles = [10, 25, 50, 75, 90, 95, 99]
for p in percentiles:
    value = np.percentile(spend, p)
    print(f"  {p}th percentile: ${value:,.2f}")
```

The `df.describe()` method gives you a quick summary that includes key percentiles:


```
print(spend.describe())
```

This single command returns the count, mean, standard deviation, minimum, 25th percentile, median, 75th percentile, and maximum. It is often the very first thing you run when exploring a new dataset, and it gives you a remarkable amount of information in a compact format.

### Z-Scores: How Many Standard Deviations Away?

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a particular value is from the mean. It standardizes values so you can compare observations across different scales.

The formula is simple: $z = \frac{x - \text{mean}}{\text{std}}$


```
from scipy import stats

z_scores = stats.zscore(spend)
print("Z-scores for annual_spend:")
for cust_id, z in zip(df["customer_id"], z_scores):
    print(f"  Customer {cust_id}: z = {z:.2f}")
```

A z-score of 0 means the value equals the mean. A z-score of 2.0 means the value is two standard deviations above the mean. In a normal distribution, about 95% of values have z-scores between -2 and +2, so values outside that range are unusual and worth investigating.

Z-scores are particularly useful for outlier detection. A common rule of thumb is to flag observations with $|z| > 3$ as potential outliers, though this threshold depends on context.


```
# Flag potential outliers
outlier_threshold = 3
outliers = df[np.abs(z_scores) > outlier_threshold]
print(f"\nPotential outliers (|z| > {outlier_threshold}): {len(outliers)} found")
```

## Distribution Shape

Two datasets can have the same mean and standard deviation but look entirely different when you plot them. The shape of a distribution tells you crucial information that summary statistics alone can miss.

### Skewness

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution. A perfectly symmetric distribution (like the normal distribution) has a skewness of zero.

* **Positive skew (right skew):** The right tail is longer. Most values cluster on the left, with a few extreme values pulling to the right. Income distributions are a classic example.
* **Negative skew (left skew):** The left tail is longer. Most values cluster on the right, with a few low values pulling to the left. Exam scores in an easy class might be negatively skewed.


```
from scipy.stats import skew, kurtosis

# Generate some skewed data
np.random.seed(42)
right_skewed = np.random.exponential(scale=50, size=1000)
symmetric = np.random.normal(loc=100, scale=15, size=1000)
left_skewed = 200 - np.random.exponential(scale=50, size=1000)

print(f"Right-skewed data: skewness = {skew(right_skewed):.2f}")
print(f"Symmetric data:    skewness = {skew(symmetric):.2f}")
print(f"Left-skewed data:  skewness = {skew(left_skewed):.2f}")
```

When skewness is present, the mean and median diverge. For right-skewed data, the mean is greater than the median (the mean gets pulled toward the long right tail). For left-skewed data, the mean is less than the median. This is why comparing the mean and median is a quick test for skewness.

### Kurtosis and Heavy Tails

Kurtosis measures how much weight is in the tails of a distribution compared to a normal distribution. High kurtosis means heavier tails and a sharper peak. Low kurtosis means lighter tails and a flatter peak.


```
print(f"Right-skewed kurtosis: {kurtosis(right_skewed):.2f}")
print(f"Symmetric kurtosis:    {kurtosis(symmetric):.2f}")

# Heavy-tailed distribution (t-distribution with low df)
heavy_tailed = np.random.standard_t(df=3, size=1000)
print(f"Heavy-tailed kurtosis: {kurtosis(heavy_tailed):.2f}")
```

The `kurtosis()` function from scipy uses excess kurtosis, meaning a normal distribution has kurtosis of 0 (not 3). A positive excess kurtosis indicates heavier tails than normal, and a negative value indicates lighter tails.

Why does this matter? Heavy-tailed distributions produce extreme values more frequently than you would expect from a normal distribution. In finance, this is critical: stock returns have heavier tails than the normal distribution predicts, which means "once-in-a-century" crashes happen much more often than simple models suggest. In data science, heavy tails mean that outliers are not anomalies to be removed but features of the underlying process.

### Visualizing Distributions

Numbers tell you the story. Plots let you see it. Always visualize your data alongside computing summary statistics.


```
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4))

axes[0].hist(right_skewed, bins=40, edgecolor="white", alpha=0.7)
axes[0].axvline(np.mean(right_skewed), color="red", linestyle="--", label="Mean")
axes[0].axvline(np.median(right_skewed), color="blue", linestyle="--", label="Median")
axes[0].set_title("Right Skewed")
axes[0].legend()

axes[1].hist(symmetric, bins=40, edgecolor="white", alpha=0.7)
axes[1].axvline(np.mean(symmetric), color="red", linestyle="--", label="Mean")
axes[1].axvline(np.median(symmetric), color="blue", linestyle="--", label="Median")
axes[1].set_title("Symmetric")
axes[1].legend()

axes[2].hist(left_skewed, bins=40, edgecolor="white", alpha=0.7)
axes[2].axvline(np.mean(left_skewed), color="red", linestyle="--", label="Mean")
axes[2].axvline(np.median(left_skewed), color="blue", linestyle="--", label="Median")
axes[2].set_title("Left Skewed")
axes[2].legend()

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

### Box Plots: A Five-Number Summary in One Picture

Box plots are one of the most information-dense visualizations in data science. A single box plot shows you the median, Q1, Q3, the IQR, and potential outliers.


```
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 5))

data_to_plot = [right_skewed, symmetric, left_skewed]
bp = ax.boxplot(data_to_plot, labels=["Right Skewed", "Symmetric", "Left Sked"], patch_artist=True)

ax.set_title("Box Plot Comparison")
ax.set_ylabel("Value")
plt.show()
```

The box spans from Q1 to Q3 (the IQR). The line inside the box is the median. The whiskers extend to the most extreme data points within $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ of the box edges. Points beyond the whiskers are plotted individually as potential outliers. This convention is not a formal statistical test for outliers, but it serves as a helpful visual flag.

## The Descriptive Statistics Toolkit: df.describe() and Beyond

Pandas gives you a powerful shortcut for computing many descriptive statistics at once. Let us work with a slightly larger dataset to see this in action.


```
np.random.seed(42)
n = 500

sales_data = pd.DataFrame({
    "revenue": np.random.lognormal(mean=7, sigma=0.8, size=n),
    "units_sold": np.random.poisson(lam=25, size=n),
    "customer_rating": np.random.choice([1, 2, 3, 4, 5], size=n, p=[0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.35, 0.30]),
    "region": np.random.choice(["North", "South", "East", "West"], size=n)
})

print(sales_data.describe())
```

The `.describe()` output gives you count, mean, std, min, 25%, 50%, 75%, and max for every numeric column. For a quick health check on a new dataset, this is invaluable. But do not stop there.


```
# Include non-numeric columns
print(sales_data.describe(include="all"))
```

Adding `include="all"` gives you categorical summaries too: count, unique values, the most frequent category (top), and its frequency (freq). This is a great way to spot data quality issues early. If a column that should have 5 categories shows 47 unique values, something is wrong.

### Going Beyond .describe()

For a more complete picture, compute additional statistics:


```
summary = pd.DataFrame({
    "mean": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].mean(),
    "median": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].median(),
    "std": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].std(),
    "skewness": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].skew(),
    "kurtosis": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].kurtosis(),
    "IQR": sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].quantile(0.75) - sales_data[["revenue", "units_sold"]].quantile(0.25)
})

print(summary.round(2))
```

This gives you a more nuanced view. If the skewness of revenue is high, you know the mean is being pulled by extreme values and the median might be a better summary. If the kurtosis is high, you know there are heavy tails and you should be cautious about assuming normality.

## Anscombe's Quartet: Why Summary Statistics Are Not Enough

In 1973, the statistician Francis Anscombe constructed four datasets that are a masterclass in why you should always visualize your data. All four datasets have nearly identical summary statistics:

* Same mean of $x$ and $y$
* Same variance of $x$ and $y$
* Same correlation between $x$ and $y$
* Same linear regression line

And yet, when you plot them, they look completely different.


```
# Anscombe's quartet manually constructed
anscombe = {
    "I":   {"x": [10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5],
            "y": [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68]},
    "II":  {"x": [10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5],
            "y": [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]},
    "III": {"x": [10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5],
            "y": [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]},
    "IV":  {"x": [8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8],
            "y": [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89]}
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 8))
for ax, (name, data) in zip(axes.flat, anscombe.items()):
    ax.scatter(data["x"], data["y"], s=50)
    ax.set_title(f"Dataset {name}")
    ax.set_xlim(3, 20)
    ax.set_ylim(2, 14)

plt.suptitle("Anscombe's Quartet: Same Stats, Different Data", fontsize=14)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Dataset I shows a classic linear relationship. Dataset II shows a clear nonlinear pattern. Dataset III has a perfect linear relationship except for one outlier that dramatically changes the regression. Dataset IV has all points at the same x-value except one extreme point that creates the illusion of a trend.

The lesson is simple and profound: **never trust summary statistics alone.** Always plot your data. The numbers give you a starting point; the plots give you the truth.

This principle is so important that it has spawned more modern variants. The Datasaurus Dozen is a collection of thirteen datasets that all share the same summary statistics but include shapes like a dinosaur, a star, and a circle when plotted. The message remains the same: visualize, visualize, visualize.

## Putting It All Together: A Descriptive Statistics Workflow

Let us walk through a realistic workflow for describing a new dataset. This is the process you should follow every time you encounter unfamiliar data.


```
np.random.seed(42)
n = 1000

customer_data = pd.DataFrame({
    "age": np.random.normal(loc=38, scale=12, size=n).clip(18, 80).astype(int),
    "monthly_spend": np.random.lognormal(mean=4.5, sigma=0.9, size=n),
    "visits_per_month": np.random.poisson(lam=8, size=n),
    "satisfaction": np.random.choice(
        ["Very Unhappy", "Unhappy", "Neutral", "Happy", "Very Happy"],
        size=n, p=[0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.35, 0.25]
    ),
    "churn": np.random.binomial(1, 0.15, size=n)
})

# Step 1: Quick overview
print("Shape:", customer_data.shape)
print("\nData types:")
print(customer_data.dtypes)
print("\nFirst few rows:")
print(customer_data.head())
```


```
# Step 2: Summary statistics
print("\nNumeric summary:")
print(customer_data.describe().round(2))
print("\nFull summary (including categorical):")
print(customer_data.describe(include="all").round(2))
```


```
# Step 3: Check for skewness
print("\nSkewness:")
print(customer_data[["age", "monthly_spend", "visits_per_month"]].skew().round(2))
print("\nKurtosis:")
print(customer_data[["age", "monthly_spend", "visits_per_month"]].kurtosis().round(2))
```


```
# Step 4: Visualize distributions
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4))

for ax, col in zip(axes, ["age", "monthly_spend", "visits_per_month"]):
    ax.hist(customer_data[col], bins=30, edgecolor="white", alpha=0.7)
    ax.axvline(customer_data[col].mean(), color="red", linestyle="--", label=f"Mean: {customer_data[col].mean():.1f}")
    ax.axvline(customer_data[col].median(), color="blue", linestyle="--", label=f"Median: {customer_data[col].median():.1f}")
    ax.set_title(col)
    ax.legend(fontsize=9)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```


```
# Step 5: Group comparisons
print("\nSpend by satisfaction level:")
print(customer_data.groupby("satisfaction")["monthly_spend"].agg(["mean", "median", "std", "count"]).round(2))
```

This workflow—shape, types, summary stats, skewness, visualization, and group comparisons—gives you a comprehensive description of any dataset in just a few minutes. It catches data quality issues, reveals unexpected patterns, and sets you up for more sophisticated analysis.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Reporting Mean Without Checking Shape

If someone tells you "the average response time is 200ms," ask about the distribution shape. If response times are right-skewed (they almost always are), the median might be 120ms and most users experience much faster responses than the mean suggests. The mean is being inflated by a few very slow requests.

### Pitfall 2: Ignoring Sample Size

A standard deviation computed from 5 observations is very different from one computed from 5,000 observations. With small samples, your summary statistics are noisy estimates of the true values. Always report the sample size alongside your statistics.

### Pitfall 3: Comparing Standard Deviations Across Different Scales

Saying "revenue has a standard deviation of $500 and satisfaction has a standard deviation of 1.2" does not mean revenue is more variable. The coefficient of variation (CV) lets you make fair comparisons across different scales.

### Pitfall 4: Using df.describe() as Your Entire Analysis

The `.describe()` method is a starting point, not a destination. It does not show you skewness, kurtosis, correlations between variables, or the shape of distributions. Always follow up with visualizations and additional statistics.

## Summary

Descriptive statistics are the foundation of every data analysis. In this lesson, you have learned the essential vocabulary for describing data:

* **Data types determine constraints:** Know whether your data is numeric or categorical, discrete or continuous, nominal or ordinal to apply the right tools.
* **Central tendency summarizes typicality:** The mean balances the data but tracks outliers; the median splits it robustly. Always compare them.
* **Spread registers variation:** Variance, standard deviation, IQR, and range track how scattered data points are. Adjust them to account for your scaling units via the coefficient of variation.
* **Shape profiles asymmetry:** Skewness measures tail bias, while kurtosis logs tail density weight. High kurtosis warns of frequent extreme values.
* **Quantiles and z-scores track position:** Use them to describe exact data ranks or screen for anomalies beyond three standard deviations.
* **Visualization is essential:** Anscombe's quartet demonstrates that identical statistics can describe completely different datasets. Always plot your data.
