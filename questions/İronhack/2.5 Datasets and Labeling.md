# Datasets and Labeling

## Introduction

Every ML model learns from data, but data does not arrive labeled, balanced, and ready to use. Someone — or some system — has to decide which data to include, how to sample it, and how to assign labels. These decisions are not administrative overhead. They are engineering choices that shape what your model can learn, what biases it inherits, and how well it generalizes to the real world. A model trained on a biased sample will produce biased predictions, no matter how sophisticated the algorithm is.

This lesson treats dataset creation as a system process: a series of deliberate decisions about data selection, sampling strategy, labeling methodology, and quality control. We will examine how different sampling methods introduce or prevent bias, how labeling works in practice (both by humans and by the system itself), and how the length of feedback loops determines how quickly your model can learn from its own predictions. Along the way, you will develop the judgment to look at a training dataset and ask the right questions: Where did this data come from? How was it sampled? Who labeled it? And what assumptions are baked in?

## Sampling: Choosing What Your Model Sees

Sampling is one of the most underappreciated steps in the ML workflow. It happens at multiple stages: sampling from all possible real-world data to create a dataset, sampling from that dataset to create training/validation/test splits, and sampling from production events for monitoring. The method you choose determines whether your training data represents the real world or a distorted version of it.

### Nonprobability Sampling: Convenient but Biased

When the selection of data is not based on statistical probability criteria, we call it **nonprobability sampling**. These methods are common in practice because they are easy, but they come with systematic biases that can silently undermine your model.

* **Convenience sampling:** Selects data based on what is readily available. This is by far the most common sampling method in ML, and often not by choice. Language models are often trained on Wikipedia, Common Crawl, and Reddit — not because these are representative of all human language, but because they are freely available at scale. The result is models that perform well on internet-style text but struggle with legal documents, medical notes, or conversational speech.
* **Snowball sampling:** Starts with a small seed set and expands by following connections. To build a dataset of legitimate social media accounts, you might start with a handful of verified accounts, then collect all accounts they follow, then all accounts those accounts follow. This method is useful for discovering networks but inherits the biases of whatever seed you start with.
* **Judgment sampling:** Relying on experts to decide what data to include. A domain expert selects examples they believe are representative. This can be efficient for specialized domains, but the selection reflects the expert's mental model, which may not capture the full distribution of real-world cases.
* **Quota sampling:** Setting targets for specific subgroups without randomization. For a survey, you might require 100 responses from each age group (under 30, 30-60, over 60) regardless of the actual population distribution. This guarantees representation of each group but does not ensure that the individuals within each group are randomly selected.

The fundamental problem with all nonprobability methods is that the resulting samples are not statistically representative of the population. Models trained on them learn the patterns of the sample, not the patterns of the real world. Let's see this concretely:


```
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

np.random.seed(42)

# True population: 70% urban, 30% rural customers
population = pd.DataFrame({
    "customer_id": range(10000),
    "location": np.random.choice(["urban", "rural"], 10000, p=[0.7, 0.3]),
    "spending": np.where(
        np.random.choice(["urban", "rural"], 10000, p=[0.7, 0.3]) == "urban",
        np.random.normal(100, 30, 10000),
        np.random.normal(60, 20, 10000)
    ),
})

# Convenience sample: only data from the company's app (biased toward urban users)
convenience_sample = population[population["location"] == "urban"].sample(500, random_state=42)

print(f"True population urban/rural ratio: {(population['location']=='urban').mean():.1%} / {(population['location']=='rural').mean():.1%}")
print(f"Convenience sample urban/rural ratio: {(convenience_sample['location']=='urban').mean():.1%} / {(convenience_sample['location']=='rural').mean():.1%}")
print(f"\nTrue population avg spending: ${population['spending'].mean():.2f}")
print(f"Convenience sample avg spending: ${convenience_sample['spending'].mean():.2f}")
```

The convenience sample completely misses rural customers, giving a distorted picture of average spending. A model trained on this sample would learn that all customers behave like urban customers — a dangerous assumption for a business that serves both populations.

### Simple Random Sampling

The most straightforward probability-based method gives every item in the population an equal chance of being selected:


```
# Simple random sample: every customer has equal probability
random_sample = population.sample(500, random_state=42)

print(f"Random sample urban/rural ratio: {(random_sample['location']=='urban').mean():.1%} / {(random_sample['location']=='rural').mean():.1%}")
print(f"Random sample avg spending: ${random_sample['spending'].mean():.2f}")
```

Simple random sampling is fair and unbiased in expectation, but it has a drawback: rare categories may be underrepresented or entirely absent. If a class appears in only 0.01% of your population and you sample 1%, you are unlikely to capture any examples of that class. For tasks where rare events matter (fraud detection, disease screening), this is a serious problem.

### Stratified Sampling

Stratified sampling addresses the rare-class problem by dividing the population into meaningful groups (strata) and sampling from each group separately:


```
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

# Add a rare category to demonstrate the problem
population["risk_level"] = np.random.choice(
    ["low", "medium", "high"],
    10000,
    p=[0.90, 0.08, 0.02]  # "high" is rare: only 2%
)

# Simple random sample might miss "high" risk
simple_sample = population.sample(100, random_state=42)
print("Simple random sample risk distribution:")
print(simple_sample["risk_level"].value_counts())

# Stratified sample guarantees representation of each group
stratified_sample = population.groupby("risk_level", group_keys=False).apply(
    lambda x: x.sample(min(len(x), max(1, int(100 * len(x) / len(population)))), random_state=42))
print("\nStratified sample risk distribution:")
print(stratified_sample["risk_level"].value_counts())
```

Stratified sampling ensures that even rare classes are represented in your training data. This is critical for ML tasks where the minority class is the one you care most about. However, stratified sampling requires you to know the groups in advance, and it becomes complex when samples can belong to multiple groups (multilabel problems).

### Weighted Sampling

Weighted sampling assigns each sample a probability weight, giving you fine-grained control over the selection process:


```
import random

# Weighted sampling: give recent data higher probability
population["days_ago"] = np.random.randint(1, 365, 10000)

# Weight more recent data higher
population["weight"] = 1.0 / (population["days_ago"] + 1)
population["weight"] = population["weight"] / population["weight"].sum()

weighted_sample = population.sample(500, weights="weight", random_state=42)

print(f"Population median days_ago: {population['days_ago'].median():.0f}")
print(f"Weighted sample median days_ago: {weighted_sample['days_ago'].median():.0f}")
```

The weighted sample skews toward more recent data, which is often desirable when data distributions change over time. You can also use weighted sampling to correct known biases: if your dataset overrepresents one group, you can give underrepresented groups higher weights to compensate.

> **Sample Weights during Training:** While weighted sampling determines which data your model trains on, sample weights determine how much influence each training example has on the loss function. Samples with higher weights affect the model's decision boundary more strongly. This distinction matters: weighted sampling changes the input distribution, while sample weights change how the model learns from a fixed distribution.

### Reservoir Sampling: Sampling from Streams

In production, data often arrives as a continuous stream. You want to maintain a representative sample of size $k$ from the stream, but you do not know how many items the stream will contain. Reservoir sampling solves this elegantly:


```
import random

def reservoir_sample(stream, k):
    """Maintain a random sample of size k from a stream of unknown length.
    Every element in the stream has equal probability of being in the reservoir."""
    reservoir = []

    for i, item in enumerate(stream):
        if i < k:
            # Fill the reservoir with the first k items
            reservoir.append(item)
        else:
            # Replace elements with decreasing probability
            j = random.randint(0, i)
            if j < k:
                reservoir[j] = item
    return reservoir

# Simulate a stream of 10,000 events
random.seed(42)
stream = range(10000)
sample = reservoir_sample(stream, k=100)

print(f"Reservoir size: {len(sample)}")
print(f"Min: {min(sample)}, Max: {max(sample)}")
print(f"Mean: {sum(sample)/len(sample):.0f} (expected ~5000 for uniform stream)")
```

The algorithm is simple but mathematically rigorous. At any point in the stream, every element seen so far has an equal $k/n$ probability of being in the reservoir. Reservoir sampling is essential for production monitoring, where you need to maintain a representative sample of predictions or events without storing the entire stream.

### Importance Sampling

Importance sampling allows you to sample from one distribution when you actually need samples from a different distribution. If sampling from your target distribution $P(x)$ is expensive or impossible, you can sample from a proposal distribution $Q(x)$ instead and reweight the samples:

$$\text{Weight} = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$$

In expectation, this produces the same results as sampling from $P(x)$ directly. Importance sampling is used in reinforcement learning (estimating the value of a new policy using data from an old policy) and in various Monte Carlo methods.

## Labeling: The Foundation of Supervised Learning

Most ML models in production today are supervised, meaning they learn from labeled examples. The quality and quantity of labels directly determines model performance. Yet labeling is often treated as a mundane task rather than the critical engineering challenge it actually is.

### Hand Labels: Expensive, Slow, and Essential

Hand labeling means having humans annotate data. This is the most direct way to obtain labels, but it comes with significant challenges:

* **Cost scales with expertise:** Labeling whether a comment is spam can be done by crowdsourced annotators trained in 15 minutes. Labeling chest X-rays requires board-certified radiologists whose time is expensive and limited.
* **Speed is often the bottleneck:** Phonetic-level transcription of speech takes roughly 400 times longer than the utterance itself. One hour of speech requires 400 hours of annotation — nearly three months of full-time work.
* **Privacy constraints limit access:** Medical records, financial data, and other sensitive information may not be allowed to leave the organization, requiring secure, internal annotation teams rather than third-party public services.
* **Adaptability is poor:** If your task changes — say your sentiment model needs a new `ANGRY` class in addition to `POSITIVE` and `NEGATIVE` — all existing data must be re-examined and potentially relabeled.


```
# Simulating the cost and time of different labeling approaches
labeling_scenarios = [
    {"task": "Spam detection", "annotators": "Crowdsourced", "cost_per_label": 0.05,     "labels_per_hour": 200, "expertise": "Low"},
    {"task": "Medical image classification", "annotators": "Radiologists", "cost_per_label": 5.00,     "labels_per_hour": 20, "expertise": "High"},
    {"task": "Named entity recognition", "annotators": "Linguists", "cost_per_label": 0.50,     "labels_per_hour": 50, "expertise": "Medium"},
    {"task": "Speech transcription", "annotators": "Transcribers", "cost_per_label": 2.00,     "labels_per_hour": 10, "expertise": "Medium"},
]

target_labels = 10000
print(f"Cost and time to label {target_labels:,} examples:\n")

for scenario in labeling_scenarios:
    total_cost = scenario["cost_per_label"] * target_labels
    total_hours = target_labels / scenario["labels_per_hour"]
    print(f"  {scenario['task']}:")
    print(f"    Annotators: {scenario['annotators']} ({scenario['expertise']} expertise)")
    print(f"    Cost: ${total_cost:,.0f}")
    print(f"    Time: {total_hours:,.0f} hours ({total_hours/8:,.0f} work days)")
    print()
```

### Label Multiplicity: When Annotators Disagree

When multiple annotators label the same data, they often disagree. This is not a failure of the annotation process — it reflects genuine ambiguity in the data. The challenge is: when annotators disagree, what is the "correct" label?

Consider a named entity recognition task where three annotators label the same text:


```
text = "Darth Sidious, known simply as the Emperor, was a Dark Lord of the Sith who reigned over the galaxy as Galactic Emperor of the First Galactic Empire."

annotations = {
    "Annotator 1": [
        "Darth Sidious",
        "Dark Lord of the Sith",
        "Galactic Emperor of the First Galactic Empire"
    ],
    "Annotator 2": [
        "Darth Sidious",
        "Emperor",
        "Dark Lord",
        "Sith",
        "Galactic Emperor",
        "First Galactic Empire"
    ],
    "Annotator 3": [
        "Darth Sidious",
        "Emperor",
        "Dark Lord of the Sith",
        "Galactic Emperor of the First Galactic Empire"
    ],
}

for annotator, entities in annotations.items():
    print(f"{annotator}: {len(entities)} entities")
    for e in entities:
        print(f"    [{e}]")
    print()

# Check agreement
all_entities = set()
for entities in annotations.values():
    all_entities.update(entities)

print(f"Total unique entities across annotators: {len(all_entities)}")
print(f"Entities all three agree on: ", end="")
common = set(annotations["Annotator 1"]) & set(annotations["Annotator 2"]) & set(annotations["Annotator 3"])
print(common)
```

Annotator 1 found 3 entities, Annotator 2 found 6, and Annotator 3 found 4. They all agree on `"Darth Sidious"` but disagree on everything else. Should `"Galactic Emperor of the First Galactic Empire"` be one entity or two? A model trained on Annotator 1's labels will behave very differently from one trained on Annotator 2's labels.

To minimize disagreement, you need clear annotation guidelines that address edge cases, thorough training for annotators, and a resolution process for conflicts (majority vote, expert adjudication, or treating disagreement itself as a signal of genuine ambiguity).

### Data Lineage: Tracking Where Labels Come From

When you combine data from multiple sources with different annotators, quality varies. If you cannot trace which annotator labeled which sample, debugging model failures becomes nearly impossible.


```
# Tracking data lineage for labeled samples
labeled_data = pd.DataFrame({
    "sample_id": range(1, 11),
    "text": [
        "Great product, love it!", "Terrible, waste of money",
        "It's okay, nothing special", "Best purchase ever!!!",
        "Completely broken on arrival", "Decent quality for the price",
        "Would not recommend", "Five stars, amazing",
        "Meh, could be better", "Absolutely fantastic"
    ],
    "label": ["positive", "negative", "neutral", "positive", "negative",
              "positive", "negative", "positive", "neutral", "positive"],
    "annotator_id": ["ann_01", "ann_01", "ann_02", "ann_01", "ann_03",
                     "ann_02", "ann_03", "ann_01", "ann_02", "ann_03"],
    "source": ["batch_1", "batch_1", "batch_1", "batch_1", "batch_2",
               "batch_2", "batch_2", "batch_2", "batch_2", "batch_2"],
    "annotation_date": pd.to_datetime([
        "2025-01-10", "2025-01-10", "2025-01-11", "2025-01-10", "2025-02-15",
        "2025-02-15", "2025-02-16", "2025-01-10", "2025-02-15", "2025-02-16"
    ]),
})

# Analyze by annotator
print("Labels per annotator:")
print(labeled_data.groupby("annotator_id")["label"].value_counts().unstack(fill_value=0))

print("\nLabels per source batch:")
print(labeled_data.groupby("source")["label"].value_counts().unstack(fill_value=0))
```

Data lineage lets you ask questions like: *"Did the model's accuracy drop because of a specific batch of labels?"* or *"Does one annotator consistently skew the distribution?"* Without lineage tracking, debugging data processing becomes pure guesswork.

## Natural Labels: When the System Provides Feedback

Not all labels need to come from human annotators. For some tasks, the system itself can infer labels from user behavior or real-world outcomes. These are **natural labels**, and they are highly valuable in production data architectures.

### Behavioral Labels from User Actions

Recommender systems are the canonical example. When you recommend a product and the user clicks on it, that click is a positive signal. When the user ignores the recommendation, that is a negative signal. These behavioral labels are generated automatically, at scale, without any human annotation effort.


```
# Simulating natural labels from a recommender system
np.random.seed(42)

recommendations = pd.DataFrame({
    "rec_id": range(1, 1001),
    "user_id": np.random.randint(1, 101, 1000),
    "item_id": np.random.randint(1, 501, 1000),
    "timestamp": pd.date_range("2025-03-01", periods=1000, freq="min"),
    "was_clicked": np.random.choice([True, False], 1000, p=[0.15, 0.85]),
})

# After a wait window, unclicked recommendations become negative labels
recommendations["label"] = recommendations["was_clicked"].map({True: "positive", False: "negative"})

print(f"Total recommendations: {len(recommendations)}")
print(f"Clicked (positive): {recommendations['label'].value_counts()['positive']}")
print(f"Not clicked (negative): {recommendations['label'].value_counts()['negative']}")
print(f"Click-through rate: {recommendations['was_clicked'].mean():.1%}")
```

Many tasks can be framed to take advantage of natural labels: ad click prediction, search ranking, content feed ordering, and email spam detection (users marking messages as spam) all generate behavioral feedback that serves as explicit or implicit ground truth.

### Feedback Loop Length

For tasks with natural labels, a critical parameter is the **feedback loop length**: the time between when a prediction is served and when feedback arrives. This determines how quickly your model can learn from its own mistakes.

| Task | Feedback Loop | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ad click prediction** | Seconds to minutes | User clicks (or doesn't) almost immediately |
| **Product recommendation** | Minutes to hours | User may browse for a while before clicking/buying |
| **Content engagement** | Hours | User reads the article or watches a video |
| **Apparel recommendation** | Weeks | Items must be shipped, tried on, and kept/returned |
| **Fraud detection** | Months | Chargeback/dispute window is typically 1-3 months |
| **Loan default prediction** | Months to years | Defaults may not happen for a long time after approval |

Short feedback loops let you detect and fix model issues within hours. Long feedback loops mean that a broken model might cause damage for months before the ground truth reveals the drop in performance.

## Handling the Lack of Labels

When hand labels are too expensive, too slow, or insufficient, several engineering techniques can help you build a working model with limited labeled data.

### Weak Supervision: Encoding Expertise as Functions

Weak supervision replaces hand labels with **labeling functions (LFs)**: programmatic rules that encode domain heuristics. If you can capture an expert's decision rules in code, you can label data at scale.


```
# Labeling functions for classifying support tickets as urgent or not

def lf_keyword_urgent(text):
    """If the text contains urgent keywords, label as URGENT."""
    urgent_words = ["crash", "down", "emergency", "outage", "broken", "critical"]
    text_lower = text.lower()
    if any(word in text_lower for word in urgent_words):
        return "URGENT"
    return None  # Abstain if no match

def lf_keyword_routine(text):
    """If the text contains routine keywords, label as ROUTINE."""
    routine_words = ["question", "how to", "wondering", "curious", "feature request"]
    text_lower = text.lower()
    if any(word in text_lower for word in routine_words):
        return "ROUTINE"
    return None

def lf_exclamation_marks(text):
    """Multiple exclamation marks suggest urgency."""
    if text.count("!") >= 3:
        return "URGENT"
    return None

def lf_length(text):
    """Very short tickets are often routine questions."""
    if len(text.split()) < 5:
        return "ROUTINE"
    return None

# Apply labeling functions to support tickets
tickets = [
    "The production server is down and nothing works!!!",
    "How to reset my password?",
    "Critical outage affecting all users in EU region",
    "I'm curious about the new reporting feature",
    "Everything crashed after the latest update!!!!",
    "Quick question about billing",
    "System broken, customers cannot check out",
    "Feature request: dark mode for the dashboard",
]

labeling_functions = [lf_keyword_urgent, lf_keyword_routine, lf_exclamation_marks, lf_length]

print(f"{'Ticket':<55} {'LF1':>8} {'LF2':>8} {'LF3':>8} {'LF4':>8}")
print("-" * 95)
for ticket in tickets:
    labels = [lf(ticket) or "—" for lf in labeling_functions]
    print(f"{ticket[:54]:<55} {labels[0]:>8} {labels[1]:>8} {labels[2]:>8} {labels[3]:>8}")
```

Each labeling function captures one heuristic. Some functions may agree, some may conflict, and many will abstain (`None`). The power of weak supervision (using frameworks like Snorkel) comes from combining these multiple noisy LFs using statistical models that learn their programmatic reliability and output a single probabilistic label.

### Semi-Supervised Learning: Leveraging Structure

Semi-supervised learning starts with a small set of labeled data and uses the underlying distribution of the unlabeled data to expand labels. A common approach is **self-training**: train a model on labeled data, predict labels for unlabeled samples, add high-confidence predictions to the training set, and repeat.


```
# Simulating self-training for semi-supervised learning
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification

np.random.seed(42)

# Generate a dataset where only 5% is labeled
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=2000, n_features=10, n_informative=5,
                          n_classes=2, random_state=42)

n_labeled = 100
labeled_mask = np.zeros(len(y), dtype=bool)
labeled_mask[:n_labeled] = True
np.random.shuffle(labeled_mask)

X_labeled = X[labeled_mask]
y_labeled = y[labeled_mask]
X_unlabeled = X[~labeled_mask]
y_true_unlabeled = y[~labeled_mask]

# Self-training step
model = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=50, random_state=42)
model.fit(X_labeled, y_labeled)

initial_acc = model.score(X_unlabeled, y_true_unlabeled)
print(f"Initial model accuracy (trained on {n_labeled} labels): {initial_acc:.3f}")

# Take high-confidence predictions as pseudo-labels
proba = model.predict_proba(X_unlabeled)
confidence = proba.max(axis=1)
high_confidence = confidence > 0.9
pseudo_labels = model.predict(X_unlabeled[high_confidence])

# Retrain with expanded dataset
X_expanded = np.vstack([X_labeled, X_unlabeled[high_confidence]])
y_expanded = np.concatenate([y_labeled, pseudo_labels])

model_v2 = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=50, random_state=42)
model_v2.fit(X_expanded, y_expanded)

improved_acc = model_v2.score(X_unlabeled[~high_confidence], y_true_unlabeled[~high_confidence])
print(f"Improved model accuracy on remaining data: {improved_acc:.3f}")
```

### Transfer Learning & Active Learning

* **Transfer learning:** Reuses a network trained on an abundant, general task (e.g., language modeling on billions of words) as the starting point for a specific downstream task. Fine-tuning a pretrained model requires far fewer task-specific labels to achieve high accuracy.
* **Active learning:** Flips the labeling process. Instead of randomly selecting data to label, the model chooses which unlabeled samples would be most informative if annotated (e.g., using *uncertainty sampling* to find examples near the decision boundary).


```
# Active Learning concept: find the samples the model is most uncertain about
proba_pool = model.predict_proba(X_unlabeled[:500])
uncertainty = 1 - proba_pool.max(axis=1)
most_informative_indices = np.argsort(uncertainty)[-20:]

print(f"Avg confidence of selected uncertain samples for human review: {proba_pool.max(axis=1)[most_informative_indices].mean():.3f}")
```

## Dataset Bias: Seeing What Is Not There

Every sampling and labeling choice introduces potential bias that a model will inherit and amplify:

* **Selection bias:** Occurs when your sample is not representative of the true production population (e.g., self-driving car data collected only in sunny cities struggling in snow).
* **Labeling bias:** Occurs when annotators' backgrounds or ambiguous guidelines systematically skew annotations.
* **Measurement bias:** Occurs when the features measured act as a problematic proxy for the actual target (e.g., using arrest records as a proxy for crime rates, which incorporates localized policing patterns).
* **Historical bias:** Embedded in data that reflects past human structural decisions or inequalities.

The primary defense against data bias is building solid tracking around **data lineage** — knowing exactly where each sample came from, how it was filtered, and who annotated it — followed by systematic validation across specific sub-populations.

## Summary

* **Sampling determines what your model sees.** Nonprobability methods are fast but prone to bias, while probability-based methods (simple random, stratified, weighted) offer statistical safety. Reservoir sampling allows you to maintain representative evaluation pools from live data streams.
* **Labeling determines what your model learns.** Hand labeling is slow and resource-heavy, running into annotator conflict. Data lineage is critical to track annotation quality over time.
* **Natural labels leverage system feedback loops.** Inferred from user actions, their value depends heavily on the feedback loop length. Short loops allow quick feature iterations; long loops require defensive validation.
* **Label scarcity can be solved programmatically.** Weak supervision scales expert heuristics using labeling functions. Semi-supervised learning, transfer learning, and active learning minimize human annotation bottlenecks by focusing resources efficiently.
