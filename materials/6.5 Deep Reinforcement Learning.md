# Deep Reinforcement Learning

## Introduction

Back in Unit 4 you met classical reinforcement learning — Q-learning, SARSA, the Bellman equation, $\epsilon$-greedy exploration. You stored Q-values in a table indexed by state and action, and slowly filled it in by playing many episodes of FrozenLake or Taxi.

That table-based approach works beautifully for tiny, discrete environments. The instant you try to apply it to anything realistic — a video game with millions of pixel states, a robot with continuous joint angles, a poker game with billions of possible hands — the table explodes. You can't store a Q-value for every possible Atari screen.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (Deep RL) solves this by replacing the Q-table with a neural network that approximates $Q(s, a)$. Or, going one step further, replaces the policy itself with a neural network that maps states to actions. Suddenly RL works on raw pixels, continuous control, and large language models.

In this lesson you'll learn:

* Why function approximation with neural networks is the natural next step after tabular RL.
* **Deep Q-Networks (DQN)** — the algorithm that taught a single agent to play 49 Atari games at human level, and the two tricks (experience replay + target network) that make it work.
* **Policy gradients** — a different family that learns the policy directly, without ever estimating Q.
* **Actor-critic methods and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** — the modern default for most real-world Deep RL.
* How Deep RL powers AlphaGo, AlphaZero, robotic control, and the RLHF step behind every modern LLM.

We'll also touch on the libraries you'd actually reach for in 2026: `Gymnasium` (the maintained fork of OpenAI Gym) and `Stable-Baselines3` for high-quality reference implementations.

> **Note:** This lesson covers classical (non-deep-learning) RL methods. Deep RL approaches like DQN and PPO will be covered in the Deep Learning unit.

## From Q-tables to Q-networks

Recall the Q-learning update rule:


```
Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · [ r + γ · max_a' Q(s', a') − Q(s, a) ]
```

In tabular Q-learning, $Q(s, a)$ is literally a cell in a 2D array. Every state-action pair gets its own number, and we update one cell at a time.

What changes when the state is an image of size $84 \times 84 \times 4$ (a stack of recent Atari frames)? There are roughly $256^{84 \cdot 84 \cdot 4} \approx 10^{68080}$ possible states. There aren't enough atoms in the universe to store that table.

The fix: parameterise $Q$ with a neural network.

```
   state s  ──►  ┌───────────────────┐  ──►  Q(s, a₁)
                 │   Q-network θ     │  ──►  Q(s, a₂)
                 │  (a neural net)   │  ──►  …
                 └───────────────────┘  ──►  Q(s, aₙ)

```

Now we don't store every cell — we store the weights $\theta$ of a network that can produce Q-values for any state we hand it. The network has, say, a million parameters. It generalises across similar states, just like a CNN generalises across similar images.

The learning question becomes: how do we train $\theta$ so that the network's outputs satisfy the Bellman equation? That's exactly what DQN does.

## Deep Q-Networks (DQN)

DQN (Mnih et al., 2013/2015) was the algorithm that made Deep RL famous. A single network, the same architecture, learned to play 49 different Atari 2600 games from raw pixels — matching or exceeding human performance on most of them.

The core idea is dead simple: treat the Bellman equation as a supervised-learning target.
For a transition $(s, a, r, s')$, the Bellman target is:

$$y = r + \gamma \cdot \max_{a'} Q_{\theta}(s', a')$$

We train the Q-network to minimise:

$$L(\theta) = ( Q_{\theta}(s, a) - y )^2$$

That's just MSE between the network's current prediction and what the Bellman equation says the prediction should be. Run gradient descent on $\theta$, repeat.

If you tried this directly, however, it would barely work. Two seemingly small additions make it work spectacularly well.

### Trick 1 — Experience Replay

In standard supervised learning, your training examples are i.i.d. — drawn independently from some fixed distribution. In RL, consecutive transitions are anything but: they come from the same trajectory, and they shift over time as the policy improves. Training a neural net on this kind of strongly correlated, non-stationary data is a recipe for catastrophe.

Experience replay breaks the correlation. Every transition $(s, a, r, s', \text{done})$ the agent experiences is stored in a large replay buffer (typically $10^5$ to $10^6$ transitions). To compute a gradient update, you sample a random mini-batch of past transitions from this buffer.


```
class ReplayBuffer:
    def __init__(self, capacity=100_000):
        self.buf = collections.deque(maxlen=capacity)

    def push(self, s, a, r, s_next, done):
        self.buf.append((s, a, r, s_next, done))

    def sample(self, batch_size):
        batch = random.sample(self.buf, batch_size)
        s, a, r, s_next, done = zip(*batch)
        return (np.array(s, dtype=np.float32),
                np.array(a, dtype=np.int64),
                np.array(r, dtype=np.float32),
                np.array(s_next, dtype=np.float32),
                np.array(done, dtype=np.float32))
```

Two big wins from replay:

1. Decorrelated batches make gradient estimates well-behaved.
2. Sample efficiency — every real interaction with the environment can drive many gradient updates.

### Trick 2 — Target Network

The second problem is even subtler. The Bellman target

$$y = r + \gamma \cdot \max_{a'} Q_{\theta}(s', a')$$

depends on the same network we're trying to update. As we change $\theta$, the target moves. You're chasing a target you yourself are pulling away. Training oscillates or diverges.

The fix: keep a separate frozen copy of the network, parameters $\theta^-$, used only for computing the target:

$$y = r + \gamma \cdot \max_{a'} Q_{\theta^-}(s', a')$$

Every few thousand steps you simply copy $\theta$ into $\theta^-$. The target is now stationary on the timescale of typical updates, and training becomes stable.

### The Full DQN Loop

Putting both tricks together:


```
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.optim as optim
import gymnasium as gym, numpy as np, random, collections

env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
n_obs = env.observation_space.shape[0]
n_act = env.action_space.n

class QNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(n_obs, 128), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, 128),   nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, n_act),
        )
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

q       = QNet()
target  = QNet()
target.load_state_dict(q.state_dict())     # θ⁻ ← θ
optim_  = optim.Adam(q.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
buffer  = ReplayBuffer(capacity=50_000)
gamma, eps = 0.99, 1.0

for step in range(50_000):
    state, _ = env.reset() if step == 0 else (state, None)

    # ε-greedy action
    if random.random() < eps:
        action = env.action_space.sample()
    else:
        with torch.no_grad():
            action = q(torch.tensor(state)).argmax().item()

    next_state, reward, term, trunc, _ = env.step(action)
    buffer.push(state, action, reward, next_state, term or trunc)
    state = next_state if not (term or trunc) else env.reset()[0]

    # Gradient step on a random mini-batch
    if len(buffer.buf) >= 1000:
        s, a, r, sn, d = buffer.sample(64)
        s, sn = torch.tensor(s), torch.tensor(sn)
        a, r, d = torch.tensor(a), torch.tensor(r), torch.tensor(d)

        with torch.no_grad():
            y = r + gamma * (1 - d) * target(sn).max(1).values

        q_pred = q(s).gather(1, a.unsqueeze(1)).squeeze(1)
        loss = (q_pred - y).pow(2).mean()
        optim_.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optim_.step()

    # Slowly sync target network
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        target.load_state_dict(q.state_dict())

    eps = max(0.05, eps * 0.9995)        # decay exploration
```

That's a complete (if minimal) DQN. You'll write a polished version of this in today's lab.

## DQN Improvements

Within a few years of the original DQN paper, the community piled on a series of small upgrades that each gave a measurable boost. You don't need to memorise the details, but it's worth knowing the names:

* **Double DQN** — fixes the systematic overestimation bias of the max in the target.
* **Dueling DQN** — splits the network into a state-value head and an advantage head.
* **Prioritised Experience Replay (PER)** — sample transitions whose Bellman error was largest, more often.
* **Noisy Nets** — replaces $\epsilon$-greedy with learnable noise injected into the weights.
* **Rainbow DQN** — combines all of the above and is essentially the modern DQN.

Whenever you see "DQN" in 2026, assume people mean at least Double DQN with replay, often with one or two more of these tricks.

## Policy Gradients: A Different Way of Looking at RL

DQN is value-based: it learns $Q$, and the policy is just "take argmax over $Q$." That works well when actions are discrete and the state space is moderate. It struggles when:

* Actions are continuous — you can't take argmax over an infinite set.
* The optimal policy is stochastic — DQN's argmax produces a deterministic policy.
* The action space is huge — the argmax becomes expensive.

Policy gradient methods take a completely different approach: parameterise the policy directly as $\pi_{\theta}(a \mid s)$, a neural network that outputs a probability distribution over actions, and update $\theta$ by gradient ascent on the expected return:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_{\theta}} [ R(\tau) ] \quad (\text{expected return of trajectories})$$

$$\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau} \left[ \sum_{t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot G_t \right]$$

This is the policy gradient theorem. Don't worry about the derivation — what matters is the recipe:

1. Roll out an episode using the current policy.
2. Look at every action you took. If the return after that action was high, increase its probability. If it was low, decrease it.
3. Repeat.

The simplest implementation is REINFORCE (Williams, 1992):


```
log_probs, rewards = [], []
state, _ = env.reset()

while not done:
    probs = policy(torch.tensor(state))
    dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(probs=probs)
    action = dist.sample()
    log_probs.append(dist.log_prob(action))
    state, reward, term, trunc, _ = env.step(action.item())
    rewards.append(reward)
    done = term or trunc

returns = discounted_returns(rewards, gamma=0.99)        # cumulative discounted
returns = (returns - returns.mean()) / (returns.std() + 1e-8)

loss = -(torch.stack(log_probs) * returns).sum()         # gradient *ascent* on J → negate for loss
optim_.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optim_.step()
```

That's the entire algorithm. It works on small problems but is notoriously high-variance — the policy can swing wildly because we're using the raw, noisy episode returns as the learning signal.

## Actor-Critic and PPO

The fix for REINFORCE's variance problem is **actor-critic**: keep the policy network (the "actor"), but add a second network that learns a value function $V(s)$ (the "critic"). Use the critic to compute a much lower-variance learning signal — the advantage $A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s)$, which says "how much better was this action than average?"

```
   state s  ──►  ┌─────────┐  ──►  π(· | s)      [actor]
                 │ network │
                 │   θ     │  ──►  V(s)           [critic]
                 └─────────┘
    gradient signal:  ∇log π(a | s) · A(s, a)

```

A2C / A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) are the classic actor-critic algorithms. They work well but are still finicky about hyperparameters — a bad learning rate can collapse the policy in a few updates.

### PPO — the modern workhorse

Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) is the algorithm you'll see in production Deep RL today. It's robust, simple to implement, and works well across a huge range of tasks — from CartPole to humanoid robotics to fine-tuning LLMs with RLHF.

PPO's central idea: when updating the policy, don't move too far from the previous policy in a single step. Concretely, it uses a clipped surrogate objective:

$$r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)} \quad (\text{probability ratio})$$

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min( r_t \cdot A_t, \text{clip}(r_t, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \cdot A_t ) \right]$$

The clipping (typically $\epsilon = 0.2$) forbids the policy from changing too much in one update. That single trick prevents the catastrophic collapses that plagued earlier policy-gradient methods.

In practice, you almost never implement PPO from scratch — you use Stable-Baselines3 or a similar library:


```
from stable_baselines3 import PPO

model = PPO("MlpPolicy", "CartPole-v1", verbose=1)
model.learn(total_timesteps=50_000)
model.save("ppo_cartpole")

mean_reward, _ = evaluate_policy(model, env, n_eval_episodes=10)
print(f"Mean reward: {mean_reward:.1f}")
```

Two lines of substantive code. The library handles the replay-free rollout buffer, generalised advantage estimation (GAE), value-function fitting, entropy bonuses, and clipped updates.

## A Quick Map of the Deep RL Algorithm Zoo

It's easy to get lost in algorithm acronyms. Here's a small map of the main families and when to reach for each:

| Family | Examples | Action space | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Value-based** | DQN, Rainbow | Discrete | Atari-like games, discrete control, large replay buffer OK |
| **Policy gradient** | REINFORCE | Discrete or continuous | Pedagogical / very small problems |
| **Actor-critic, on-policy** | A2C, PPO | Discrete or continuous | Default choice for most tasks; robust |
| **Actor-critic, off-policy** | DDPG, SAC, TD3 | Continuous | Continuous control (robotics), sample-efficient |
| **Model-based** | MuZero, Dreamer | Either | Sample-efficient, expensive per-step compute |

If you're starting a Deep RL project in 2026 and don't have a strong reason to do otherwise, the right default is **PPO** from Stable-Baselines3 for discrete or moderate-continuous action spaces, and **SAC** from Stable-Baselines3 for high-dimensional continuous control.

## Famous Applications

A few landmark Deep RL systems are worth knowing about, because they shape how people talk about the field.

### AlphaGo, AlphaZero, MuZero (DeepMind)

AlphaGo beat the world Go champion in 2016. It combined a policy network and value network (both deep CNNs over the board) with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). The networks guide the search; the search produces better targets for the networks. AlphaZero (2017) generalised the approach to chess, shogi, and Go using only self-play — no human games at all. MuZero (2019) went further still, learning the rules of the game from interaction. The lineage from DQN $\rightarrow$ AlphaGo $\rightarrow$ MuZero is one of the most striking achievements in modern AI.

### Robotic Locomotion and Manipulation

Deep RL is now standard in research-grade robotics. Algorithms like SAC, PPO, and recently diffusion policies have produced humanoid and quadruped robots that learn complex gaits, manipulation skills, and recovery behaviours largely in simulation, then transfer to the real robot with domain randomisation.

### RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

The post-training step that turned GPT-style language models into ChatGPT, Claude, and friends is RLHF. The recipe in three steps:

1. Start with a pretrained language model.
2. Collect a dataset of human preferences ("I prefer response A to response B"). Train a reward model on these preferences.
3. Fine-tune the language model with PPO, using the reward model as the reward signal.

The base language model is the policy, prompts are the states, generated tokens are the actions, and the reward model scores the resulting response. RLHF is why modern LLMs feel polite, helpful, and aligned compared to raw next-token-prediction models — and PPO is the algorithm in step 3. (More recent variants like DPO skip the RL step entirely, but PPO remains the textbook approach.) If you want to understand any modern conversational LLM at a technical level, you need to understand PPO.

## Practical Reality: Deep RL Is Hard

Some honest words before you dive into the lab.

Deep RL is famously fiddly. The same algorithm on the same environment with a different random seed can succeed or fail. Reward shaping, observation normalisation, batch sizes, entropy bonuses, and discount factors all matter and interact. Papers regularly report median-over-many-seeds because individual runs are so noisy.

Three pieces of pragmatic advice:

1. Start with a simple environment like CartPole or LunarLander to debug your pipeline. Don't try to solve a hard task with untested code.
2. Use a well-known library (Stable-Baselines3, CleanRL) for anything serious. Their hyperparameters have been tuned across many tasks and you won't accidentally introduce subtle bugs.
3. Plot the reward curve over many random seeds before drawing any conclusions about whether your algorithm "works".

The good news: once you've internalised the DQN and PPO recipes, every other Deep RL algorithm is a variation on one of these themes. You don't need to learn 50 algorithms — you need to understand two well.

## Summary

* Classical RL with tables breaks down whenever states or actions become large. Deep RL replaces the Q-table or the policy with a neural network, and trains it with gradient descent.
* **Deep Q-Networks (DQN)** train a Q-network with the Bellman target as a regression label. The two ingredients that make it work in practice are experience replay (decorrelate samples) and a target network (stabilise the regression target).
* DQN spawned a family — Double, Dueling, Prioritised Replay, Rainbow — each adding a small refinement on top of the same core idea.
* Policy gradient methods (REINFORCE) parameterise the policy directly and update it by gradient ascent on expected return. They handle continuous and stochastic actions but suffer from high variance.
* Actor-critic methods (A2C, A3C, PPO) cut that variance by learning a value-function critic and using it to score actions. **PPO is the modern default** — robust, simple, and widely deployed.
* For continuous control, SAC, DDPG, and TD3 are the go-to algorithms.
* Deep RL powers some of the most visible AI breakthroughs: AlphaGo / AlphaZero / MuZero, modern robotics, and the RLHF post-training step behind every aligned LLM.
* **Stable-Baselines3** gives you production-quality DQN, PPO, SAC, and TD3 in a few lines of code. Reach for it whenever you don't have a research reason to roll your own.
